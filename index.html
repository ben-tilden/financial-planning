Cash flow nets out estimated taxes (see the Taxes section for assumptions and overrides). Growth rates compound annually for income, deductions, rent, and expenses; asset growth compounds monthly. If a month's cash flow goes negative, it draws down liquid assets first (brokerage, then accessible Roth contributions) rather than letting a phantom cash balance go negative — genuine debt only shows up once every liquid account is exhausted. "Contributions only" liquidity is modeled like a Roth IRA — new contributions are immediately accessible, growth on top isn't until 59½ (a few thousand may be exempt for a first home; not modeled here). Roth 401(k)s follow stricter pro-rata rules in real life, so if you've marked one "contributions only," treat that number as optimistic. Numbers are estimates for planning, not financial or tax advice.
